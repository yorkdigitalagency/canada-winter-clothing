Down or synthetic: which should you choose?

Match insulation to wet or dry, not to marketing. Down is warmer for its weight and packs smaller, but loses over 90% of its insulation when saturated with water. Synthetic insulation is heavier and less warm per gram, but retains warmth when wet and dries faster — so it is the safer choice for wet coastal winters and high-sweat activity, while down wins for dry, deep cold.

Fill power: what it does and does not tell you

Fill power measures how much space one ounce of down fills, typically 550 to 900 cubic inches per ounce. Higher fill power means better warmth-to-weight — a 750-fill jacket can be lighter and more packable than a bulkier 600-fill piece while keeping you just as warm. But fill power alone is not warmth: total warmth depends on fill power and fill weight (how much down is used), plus construction. A high fill power jacket with little fill is still a light jacket.

Temperature ratings are brand claims, not a standard

Brands do not share one temperature-rating test. A rating is a comfort guideline from that brand, and warmth changes with activity level, wind exposure, moisture and what you layer underneath. Use ratings to compare jackets within one brand, and use cut, hood, wind protection and layering room to compare across brands. If you run cold, choose a warmer rating than the chart suggests.

The 3-layer system, step by step

  1. Base layer (against skin): moisture-wicking — moves sweat away so you do not get cold from your own moisture. No cotton.
  2. Mid-layer (insulation): down, synthetic fill or wool sweater — traps warm air. This is where most of the warmth comes from.
  3. Shell (outside): windproof and waterproof — blocks wind, rain and snow from stripping the warmth away.

Each layer has one job. Together they handle cold, wind and wet better than any single heavy coat, and you can adjust as activity and temperature change.

Waterproofing basics

Down needs a waterproof or water-resistant shell in wet conditions; synthetic copes better on its own. Durable water repellent (DWR) coatings wear off — reproof when water stops beading on the shell. For heavy rain, a dedicated waterproof layer over the insulation beats relying on the insulation's own shell.

One safety note

Environment and Climate Change Canada guidance: frostbite risk rises rapidly once wind chill drops below −27, and at −28 to −39, exposed skin is at high risk within about 30 minutes. A good hood, gloves and face coverage are safety gear at those temperatures, not accessories.

Frequently asked questions

Is down or synthetic insulation better?

Down is warmer for its weight and packs smaller, but loses over 90% of its insulation when saturated with water. Synthetic insulation is heavier and less warm per gram, but retains warmth when wet and dries faster, so it is the safer choice for wet coastal winters and high-sweat activity.

What does fill power mean?

Fill power measures how much space one ounce of down fills, from about 550 to 900 cubic inches. Higher fill power means better warmth-to-weight, not more warmth on its own. Total warmth depends on fill power and fill weight together, plus construction.

Can I trust a jacket temperature rating?

Only as a guide. Brands do not share one temperature-rating standard, and warmth changes with activity level, wind, moisture and layering. A rating tells you how the brand positions the jacket, not a measured guarantee.

What is the 3-layer system for winter?

The 3-layer system is a moisture-wicking base layer against the skin, an insulating mid-layer such as down, synthetic fill or wool, and a windproof, waterproof shell on the outside. Each layer has one job, and together they handle cold, wind and wet better than any single heavy coat.
